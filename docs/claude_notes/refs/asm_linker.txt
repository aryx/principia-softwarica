Academic references for the Assembler and Linker books
======================================================
Books covered: assemblers/Assembler.nw, assemblers/Intro.nw (included by
Assembler.nw), assemblers/Assembler_x86.nw, linkers/Linker.nw,
linkers/Intro.nw (included by Linker.nw), linkers/Linker_x86.nw
(included as an appendix of Linker.nw).

Every placement below goes on the lines immediately BEFORE the given
<<chunk>>= line. For prose-only spots in Intro.nw, the anchor is the first
words of a paragraph: put the %ref: lines immediately before that
paragraph, after any %claude:/%cs-history: tag line that opens it.

Source I checked for the Plan 9 side: compilers/docs/compiler.ms (Ken
Thompson, "Plan 9 C Compilers"). Its bibliography has [Szy78] and [Dav91]
verbatim, and it describes the loader's branch-removal pass, how it
allocates data smallest first, and its span loop. Several entries below
quote it.

### Intro.nw : paragraph "Before assemblers existed, programming meant encoding"
(line ~621, \subsection{Assemblers and assembly language}, the %claude:
%cs-history: paragraph that already names Wheeler/EDSAC 1949 in its prose)
Algorithm/idea: the founding assemblers (the first assembly notation, and
  EDSAC's Initial Orders, which translated mnemonic orders from tape)
Verified: the prose names "EDSAC ... 1949 ... David Wheeler" but has no
  \cite. The Booth report is the usual source given for the "first
  assembly language" claim.
CONFIDENCE: high (Wilkes-Wheeler-Gill); medium (Booth: exact title and
  date of the IAS report)
Proposed lines:
%ref: M. V. Wilkes, D. J. Wheeler, S. Gill, "The Preparation of Programs
%  for an Electronic Digital Computer", Addison-Wesley, 1951 (the EDSAC
%  book. Its "initial orders", written by Wheeler in 1949, were read from
%  paper tape: they turned orders punched as a letter plus a decimal
%  address, e.g. "A 100 F", into binary words in memory. That makes them
%  both the first assembler and the first loader, the two jobs 5a and 5l
%  split between them).
%ref: A. D. Booth, K. H. V. Britten, "Coding for A.R.C.", Institute for
%  Advanced Study, Princeton, 1947 (Kathleen Britten, later Booth, wrote
%  down a symbolic notation for the orders of the ARC relay computer at
%  Birkbeck. It is often called the first assembly language, although it
%  was translated by hand, not by a program).

### Intro.nw : paragraph "There is no [[CALL]] or [[RET]] instruction in the ARM."
(line ~1545, \subsection{Function calls} \label{sec:bl}; see the author's
note at line ~72 "\l subroutine concept was discovered by David Wheeler;
not that simple!")
Algorithm/idea: subroutine linkage through a return address that the
  caller hands to the callee (the "Wheeler jump"); ARM's BL/R14 is a
  hardware version of it
Verified: this paragraph and the next ones say BL saves PC+4 in R14, a
  leaf returns with B (R14), and a non-leaf must spill R14 to the stack
  (the extra word that TEXT reserves). In Wheeler's scheme the return
  address also arrives in a register (EDSAC's accumulator), but the callee
  wrote it into its own final jump instruction instead of a stack slot.
CONFIDENCE: high (the Wheeler citation is the one the coordinator supplied);
  medium (Knuth: I am sure of 1.4.1 "Subroutines", fairly sure the history
  is in 1.4.5)
Proposed lines:
%ref: D. J. Wheeler, "The use of sub-routines in programmes", Proceedings
%  of the 1952 ACM National Meeting (Pittsburgh), pp. 235-236 (EDSAC's
%  "Wheeler jump": the caller put its own address in the accumulator and
%  jumped, and the subroutine's first orders turned it into the return
%  jump planted at the subroutine's end. Like BL, the return address
%  comes in a register. Unlike 5l's non-leaf prologue, it was stored into
%  the callee's code, not on a stack, so no recursion was possible).
%ref: D. E. Knuth, "The Art of Computer Programming", Vol. 1, Section
%  1.4.1 "Subroutines" (the same linkage done on MIX with the J register,
%  which is R14 under another name. Section 1.4.5 tells the history of
%  Wheeler's invention).

### Intro.nw : paragraph "Some processors such as the x86 have a [[CALL]] instruction,"
(line ~1534, \subsection{Function calls})
Algorithm/idea: a stack of return addresses (push on call, pop on return)
Verified: the paragraph describes x86 CALL pushing the return address and
  RET popping it: the return-address stack in hardware.
CONFIDENCE: medium (that Turing's ACE report uses BURY/UNBURY for a
  return-address stack is widely repeated, and I am fairly sure of it,
  but not of the report's exact title page or date. Drop this entry if
  unsure.)
Proposed lines:
%ref: A. M. Turing, "Proposed Electronic Calculator" (the ACE report),
%  National Physical Laboratory, 1945-46 (the ACE's subroutines kept
%  their return addresses on a stack in memory, pushed by BURY and popped
%  by UNBURY. That is x86 CALL/RET done in software, years before the
%  word "stack" was in use).

### Assembler.nw : <<[[lookup()]] compute hash value [[h]] of [[symb]]>>
(line ~1528, section "[[Sym]]bols and [[hash]] table")
Algorithm/idea: a hash table with separate chaining and a prime table
  size (division method)
Verified: h = h+h+h + c (that is h = 3h + c) over the characters,
  h %= NHASH with NHASH = 503 (prime), buckets are lists chained through
  Sym.link, and a new Sym is pushed at the head of its bucket. lookup()
  compares the first character before calling memcmp.
CONFIDENCE: high (Knuth); medium (Dumey: the volume, issue and pages
  are as Knuth cites them, but I have not checked them myself)
Proposed lines:
%ref: D. E. Knuth, "The Art of Computer Programming", Vol. 3, Section 6.4
%  "Hashing" (why dividing by a prime such as NHASH = 503 spreads keys
%  well, and how chaining behaves: 5a's lists through Sym.link, with new
%  symbols pushed at the head of their bucket).
%ref: A. I. Dumey, "Indexing for rapid random access memory systems",
%  Computers and Automation 5(12), 1956 (the first published description
%  of hashing, for looking up records on a drum or disk: "divide the key
%  by a prime and use the remainder as the address", which is h %= NHASH).

### Assembler.nw : <<function [[assemble]]>>
(line ~2021, section "[[assemble()]]")
Algorithm/idea: the classic two-pass assembler (pass 1 gives labels their
  values, pass 2 emits code)
Verified: yyparse() runs twice over the same file, with pass = 1 and then
  pass = 2. In pass 1, outcode() only advances pc and label rules set
  Sym.value. In pass 2 it serializes. Difference from the textbook
  version: 5a resolves only local labels. Global symbols and relocation
  are left to 5l, so there is no relocation dictionary.
CONFIDENCE: medium (Salomon, key asl: I am sure the book covers one-pass
  vs two-pass assemblers early on, but not of the chapter number;
  Barron: sure the book exists, less sure of the 1969 edition and its
  publisher)
Proposed lines:
%ref: D. W. Barron, "Assemblers and Loaders", Macdonald, 1969 (an early
%  classic monograph. The textbook two-pass assembler, with a symbol
%  table filled in pass 1 and code produced in pass 2, is exactly 5a's
%  pass = 1 / pass = 2 around yyparse(). The textbook version also emits
%  relocation information, which 5a leaves to 5l).
%ref: D. Salomon, "Assemblers and Loaders", Ellis Horwood, 1993, ch. 1
%  (two-pass assemblers compared with one-pass ones that patch forward
%  references afterwards, the road 5a did not take).

### Assembler.nw : <<[[Opcode]] cases, branching opcodes>>   (the ARET one)
(line ~5747, \subsubsection{Function call and return} inside section
"Instructions"/"Control flow"; the author left "\t cite dijstra 1960
Recursive programming?" at line ~5734 and discusses "caller saves" at
line ~5706, both just above this chunk)
Algorithm/idea: the run-time stack for recursive procedures, and the
  caller-save register convention
Verified: the prose says that because arguments and the saved R14 live on
  the stack, and not in fixed memory cells, functions can recurse, and
  that Plan 9 uses caller-saves. RET is the virtual instruction that 5l
  expands into either B (R14) or a pop of the saved R14 into the PC. The
  Plan 9 compiler paper cites [Dav91] to justify caller-saves.
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: E. W. Dijkstra, "Recursive programming", Numerische Mathematik
%  2(1):312-318, 1960 (written for the ALGOL 60 compiler on the
%  Electrologica X1. Giving each subroutine its own fixed memory cells,
%  as EDSAC did, breaks recursion. Dijkstra kept locals, arguments and
%  links on a "stack" instead, which is what R13 and the frame reserved
%  by TEXT are).
%ref: J. W. Davidson, D. B. Whalley, "Methods for saving and restoring
%  register values across function calls", Software--Practice and
%  Experience 21(2):149-165, 1991 (measures caller-saves against
%  callee-saves. Thompson's Plan 9 compiler paper cites it for choosing
%  caller-saves, the convention quoted above).

### Assembler.nw : <<[[y.tab.c]] generated file excerpt>>
(line ~7317, section "[[yyparse()]]")
Algorithm/idea: LR(k) parsing, and LALR(1) tables as generated by Yacc
Verified: yyparse() is the table-driven LALR(1) parser that Yacc builds
  from a.y. Yacc is already \cite{yacc}/\cite{lexyacc}'d in this chapter,
  but the theory under it is not.
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: D. E. Knuth, "On the translation of languages from left to right",
%  Information and Control 8(6):607-639, 1965 (the invention of LR(k)
%  parsing: a stack automaton that "shifts" tokens and "reduces" rule
%  right-hand sides, which is what yyparse() does with the tables in
%  y.tab.c).
%ref: F. L. DeRemer, "Practical Translators for LR(k) Languages", PhD
%  thesis, MIT, 1969 (Knuth's tables were too big for the machines of the
%  day. DeRemer's LALR merges states so that they fit, and that is the
%  kind of table Yacc produces).

### Assembler.nw : <<priority and associativity declarations>>
(line ~8844, \subsection{Constant expressions})
Algorithm/idea: resolving the conflicts of an ambiguous expression
  grammar with %left precedence declarations instead of rewriting it
Verified: a.y declares %left '|', '^', '&', '<' '>', '+' '-', '*' '/' '%',
  and the expr rule is the ambiguous "expr op expr" form.
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: A. V. Aho, S. C. Johnson, J. D. Ullman, "Deterministic parsing of
%  ambiguous grammars", CACM 18(8):441-452, 1975 (why the flat
%  "expr: expr '+' expr | expr '*' expr ..." rule works: each
%  shift/reduce conflict is settled by comparing the precedence of the
%  two operators, the rule behind the %left lines here. Johnson wrote Yacc).

### Assembler.nw : <<function [[macexpand]]>>
(line ~10151, \subsection{[[#define]]} in "Other preprocessing
directives")
Algorithm/idea: macro instructions, meaning text substitution with
  parameters inside an assembler (5a is a "macro-assembler")
Verified: macdef() stores the body with formals encoded as '#' followed
  by a letter ('#a', '#b', ...). macexpand() collects the actual
  arguments (keeping track of nested parentheses, quotes and comments)
  and substitutes arg[c] for '#'+'a'+c. The caller then pushes the
  expansion back onto the input. Unlike McIlroy's macros, there is no
  macro-time computation except #ifdef, and no recursion into the body
  at definition time.
CONFIDENCE: high (McIlroy); medium (Software Tools chapter number)
Proposed lines:
%ref: M. D. McIlroy, "Macro instruction extensions of compiler languages",
%  CACM 3(4):214-220, 1960 (macros began in assemblers: one new "order"
%  expands into several with the arguments substituted, as macexpand()
%  does with its '#a' placeholders. McIlroy's macros could also test
%  conditions and call themselves recursively, which 5a's cannot).
%ref: B. W. Kernighan, P. J. Plauger, "Software Tools", Addison-Wesley,
%  1976, ch. 8 "Macro Processing" (a define/expand processor written out
%  in full: the same push-back-onto-input trick, with $1..$9 where 5a
%  uses #a..#z).

### Assembler.nw : <<function [[ieeedtod]]>>
(line ~9223, \subsection{Floating-point numbers} in "Other instructions
and registers")
Algorithm/idea: packing a double into the IEEE 754 binary64 layout
  independently of the host's float format
Verified: frexp() gives a fraction and an exponent. The code stores the
  exponent + 1022 (1023 bias minus 1, because frexp's fraction is in
  [0.5, 1)) in bits 20-30 of the high word, cuts 20 + 32 fraction bits
  with modf() while dropping the implicit leading 1 (the & 0xfffff), and
  puts the sign in bit 31. It truncates instead of rounding, and it does
  not handle denormals, infinities or NaN.
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: IEEE Standard for Binary Floating-Point Arithmetic, ANSI/IEEE Std
%  754-1985 (the double format: 1 sign bit, an 11-bit exponent with bias
%  1023, a 52-bit fraction with an implicit leading 1. That explains the
%  +1022 and & 0xfffff above. 5a builds the bit pattern by hand, so the
%  object file does not depend on the host's float format).
%ref: D. Goldberg, "What every computer scientist should know about
%  floating-point arithmetic", ACM Computing Surveys 23(1):5-48, 1991
%  (what this code leaves out: rounding, denormals, infinities, NaN).

### Intro.nw : paragraph "Another important operation done by the linker is called {\em relocation}."
(line ~713, \subsection{Relocation})
Algorithm/idea: relocating code written for address 0 to where it is
  actually loaded, plus the first subroutine library
Verified: the prose explains that JMP 30 becomes JMP 62 once the code
  sits after the header. The EDSAC initial orders were the first to do
  this: library subroutines were written as if they started at 0 and
  moved on input.
CONFIDENCE: high for the book; medium for the detail of how the initial
  orders marked relocatable addresses (hence I do not name the code
  letter)
Proposed lines:
%ref: M. V. Wilkes, D. J. Wheeler, S. Gill, "The Preparation of Programs
%  for an Electronic Digital Computer", Addison-Wesley, 1951 (EDSAC's
%  paper-tape library of subroutines, the first program library, was
%  written relative to address 0. While reading the tape, the "initial
%  orders" added the address where each subroutine was being placed,
%  which is the JMP 30 -> JMP 62 relocation of this section done in
%  1949).

### Linker.nw : <<[[lookup()]] compute hash value [[h]] of [[(symb, v)]] and [[len]]>>
(line ~831, section "[[Sym]]bols and [[hash]] table")
Algorithm/idea: chained hashing with a prime-sized table (the same code
  as 5a, with the version number folded into the key)
Verified: h starts at v (the version), then h = 3h + c; h &= 0xffffff;
  h %= NHASH. Buckets are chained through Sym.link.
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: D. E. Knuth, "The Art of Computer Programming", Vol. 3, Section 6.4
%  "Hashing" (hashing by division with chaining. Starting h at the
%  version v makes a static symbol from each file its own key, so two
%  files' "static int x" never collide in the same entry).

### Linker.nw : <<[[objfile()]] when file is a library>>
(line ~4492, section "Loading libraries manually: [[5l libxxx.a]]")
Algorithm/idea: selective loading from an archive, iterated to a fixpoint
  over the __.SYMDEF index
Verified: the while(work) loop rescans the whole symbol index and loads
  (ldobj) each member that defines a still-SXREF symbol, until a pass
  loads nothing. Unlike classic Unix ld, which scans an archive once,
  this rescanning makes the order of members inside one archive
  irrelevant, though the order of libraries on the command line still
  matters.
CONFIDENCE: medium (I am fairly sure the chapter number is 6)
Proposed lines:
%ref: J. R. Levine, "Linkers and Loaders", Morgan Kaufmann, 1999, ch. 6
%  "Libraries" (archive formats with their symbol-directory member,
%  a.out's __.SYMDEF, and why one-pass library search makes link order
%  matter. 5l's while(work) loop rescans the directory until a pass loads
%  nothing, so members within one archive can come in any order).

### Linker.nw : <<[[ldobj()]] signature compatibility check>>
(line ~4222, \subsection{[[ASIGNAME]] and [[5c -T]]} in "Safe linking")
Algorithm/idea: type-safe linkage, which catches objects compiled against
  inconsistent declarations at link time
Verified: ASIGNAME carries a 32-bit signature, computed by 5c -T from the
  type of the symbol. ldobj() reports "incompatible type signatures" when
  two objects give the same symbol different nonzero sigs. C++ gets the
  same check by putting the type into the name (mangling). Plan 9 keeps
  the name and puts a hash of the type next to it.
CONFIDENCE: high (paper and venue); pages left out
Proposed lines:
%ref: B. Stroustrup, "Type-safe linkage for C++", Computing Systems 1(4),
%  1988 (exactly the foo(int,int) vs foo(int,int,int) scenario above.
%  C++ solved it by encoding the argument types into the symbol name,
%  "name mangling", so an old caller finds no definition at all. 5c -T
%  keeps the plain name and attaches a hash of the type, so 5l can report
%  both files).

### Linker.nw : <<function [[mkfwd]]>>
(line ~5800, \subsection{Indexing [[pc]], forward links overlay}; the
author's own comment there says "called skip lists")
Algorithm/idea: express lanes of forward pointers over a sorted linked
  list, for fast search by pc (the skip-list idea)
Verified: each Prog gets at most one extra pointer, forwd. Levels
  i = LOG-1..0 take turns node by node, and level i links every cnt[i]-th
  node of its own turn. The lookup in patch() takes forwd whenever
  forwd->pc <= c, otherwise link. The structure is deterministic and
  every node has one forward arc (no tower of pointers), unlike Pugh's
  randomized skip list.
CONFIDENCE: high (Pugh); medium (Munro et al.: authors, title and SODA
  1992 are certain, pages left out)
Proposed lines:
%ref: W. Pugh, "Skip lists: a probabilistic alternative to balanced
%  trees", CACM 33(6):668-676, 1990 (a sorted linked list plus sparser
%  "express" lists above it. The search goes as far as it can on the
%  express list before stepping down. Pugh uses coin flips and a tower
%  of pointers per node. mkfwd() gives each Prog a single fixed
%  forward pointer).
%ref: J. I. Munro, T. Papadakis, R. Sedgewick, "Deterministic skip
%  lists", SODA 1992 (skip lists with the express links placed by rule
%  instead of at random, like mkfwd()'s every-cnt[i]-th node).

### Linker.nw : <<[[noops()]] second pass switch opcode cases>>   (the ATEXT one)
(line ~6223, \subsection{[[ATEXT]] patching} in "Virtual opcodes
rewriting: [[noops()]]")
Algorithm/idea: subroutine linkage: the return address stays in the link
  register for leaves and is spilled to the stack for non-leaves
Verified: for a LEAF with autosize == 0, nothing is inserted and the
  return address stays in R14. Otherwise 5l inserts MOVW.W R14,
  -autosize(R13), a push of the return address onto the stack.
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: D. J. Wheeler, "The use of sub-routines in programmes", Proceedings
%  of the 1952 ACM National Meeting (Pittsburgh), pp. 235-236 (on EDSAC
%  the return address arrived in a register and the subroutine stored it
%  into its own code, so a subroutine could not call itself. A leaf here
%  keeps R14 in the register just the same. For every other function
%  5l adds the MOVW.W push above, which makes recursion possible).
%ref: E. W. Dijkstra, "Recursive programming", Numerische Mathematik
%  2(1):312-318, 1960 (the stack discipline behind that push: each call
%  gets a fresh frame, here autosize bytes below R13).

### Linker.nw : <<function [[span]](arm)>>   (the function is dotext())
(line ~6720, section "Laying out code: [[dotext()]]"; the problem is
motivated in \subsection{Data address and code size mutual dependency},
line ~5369)
Algorithm/idea: span-dependent instruction sizing. Road not taken in 5l;
  the real iteration is 8l's span(), see Linker_x86.nw below.
Verified: dotext() does ONE pass (p->pc = c; c += oplook(p)->size),
  because on ARM a branch always takes one word and the address of a
  global is an R12 offset whose size dodata() has already fixed.
  Thompson's paper says the same about MIPS: "all instructions are one
  size. A single pass ... will determine the locations".
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: T. G. Szymanski, "Assembling code for machines with span-dependent
%  instructions", CACM 21(4):300-308, 1978 (road not taken here: when an
%  instruction's size depends on the distance it spans, sizes and
%  addresses depend on each other and need a fixpoint, which is 8l's
%  span() loop for x86. 5l avoids it with fixed-size ARM branches and
%  the R12 trick, so one pass is enough).

### Linker.nw : <<function [[oplook]](arm)>>
(line ~7569, \subsection{[[oplook()]] and [[cmp()]]}; the author asks
"\l burg?" in "Additional data structures")
Algorithm/idea: table-driven instruction selection: a rule table keyed by
  opcode and operand classes, first match wins
Verified: aclass() classifies each operand (C_REG, C_RCON, C_LCON, ...).
  oprange[as] limits the search to the rules for that opcode, and cmp()
  lets a general class match a narrower one (C_LCON covers C_RCON).
  Each Optab row gives the size and the asmout() case. It matches one
  instruction at a time, not trees, and uses no costs.
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: R. S. Glanville, S. L. Graham, "A new method for compiler code
%  generation", POPL 1978 (road not taken: the first code generator built
%  from a table describing the machine, using LR parsing to match the
%  compiler's output against the machine's instruction patterns. optab
%  is the same "machine as a table" idea, but it matches one instruction
%  at a time by operand class and takes the first rule that fits).
%ref: C. W. Fraser, R. R. Henry, T. A. Proebsting, "BURG -- fast optimal
%  instruction selection and tree parsing", SIGPLAN Notices 27(4):68-76,
%  1992 (road not taken: cost-based covering of expression trees. 5l
%  never sees trees, because 5c has already cut them into Progs).

### Linker.nw : <<function [[asmlc]]>>
(line ~12738, \subsubsection{[[asmlc()]]} in "Locations in executables",
chapter "Debugging Support", after section "Debug tables vs DWARF")
Algorithm/idea: a compact pc-to-line table coded as a byte stream of
  deltas
Verified: bytes 1-64 mean "+n lines", 65-128 mean "-n lines", 129-255
  mean "pc += (b-128)*MINLC", and 0 is followed by a 4-byte absolute
  line delta. Each line change costs about one byte. DWARF's line
  program uses the same idea (special opcodes that advance pc and line
  together), with a state machine and many more registers.
CONFIDENCE: medium (I am sure DWARF 2 was a UNIX International document
  of 1993. I believe section 6.2 is "Line Number Information", but check
  it.)
Proposed lines:
%ref: UNIX International, Programming Languages SIG, "DWARF Debugging
%  Information Format", Version 2.0, 1993, Section 6.2 (road not taken:
%  DWARF's line table is a small program for a state machine, with

### Linker.nw : <<function [[brloop]](arm)>>
(line ~14555, \subsection{Compacting chains of [[AB]], [[brloop()]]})
Algorithm/idea: branch-to-branch elimination (jump chaining), a classic
  peephole optimization
Verified: brloop() follows p->cond through consecutive AB instructions
  to the final non-B target and retargets the original branch there. It
  gives up on self-loops or after more than 5000 backward hops, which
  are infinite branch cycles.
CONFIDENCE: medium on relevance (McKeeman's 2-page paper introduced the
  peephole idea through redundant load/store pairs. I am not sure it
  discusses jump chains, hence the careful wording.)
Proposed lines:
%ref: W. M. McKeeman, "Peephole optimization", CACM 8(7):443-444, 1965
%  (the idea of sliding a small window over generated code and rewriting
%  wasteful patterns, first shown on redundant store/load pairs. Making a
%  "branch to a branch" jump straight to the final target, as brloop()
%  does, became a standard peephole rule afterwards).

### Linker.nw : <<function [[xfol]](arm)>>
(line ~14637, \subsection{Removing useless instructions, [[follow()]]})
Algorithm/idea: code layout that follows the flow of control: remove
  unconditional branches by reordering, invert conditional branches, and
  copy short tails
Verified: xfol() walks from textp. It follows AB targets to place code
  where it falls through, uses relinv() to flip a conditional branch
  when its fall-through was already placed, and copies up to 4
  instructions (the i<4 loop) instead of emitting a jump back to code
  already placed. Instructions never reached are simply not re-linked,
  so dead code disappears. Thompson's paper describes exactly this pass
  (quote below). No profile data is used.
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: K. Thompson, "Plan 9 C Compilers", Proc. Summer 1990 UKUUG
%  Conference, London, pp. 41-51 (the design note for this very function:
%  "code is reordered to remove unconditional branch instructions.
%  Conditional branch instructions are inverted ... The loader will also
%  make a copy of a few instructions to remove an unconditional branch",
%  that is xfol(), relinv(), and the i<4 copy loop).
%ref: K. Pettis, R. C. Hansen, "Profile guided code positioning", PLDI
%  1990 (road not taken: the same layout problem solved from measured
%  branch frequencies, putting the hot successor on the fall-through
%  path. xfol() has no profile and simply takes the first unplaced path).

### Linker.nw : <<[[dodata()]] in pass 1, if small data size, adjust orig>>
(line ~14505, \subsection{Small data first} in "Optimizations")
Algorithm/idea: allocate small globals first so that they fall within
  the short offset range of the static base register (R12 on ARM, R30 on
  MIPS)
Verified: globals of at most MINSIZ = 64 bytes get their addresses first
  (as SDATA1), then everything else. Thompson's paper: "External
  variables are allocated to the data segment with the smallest
  variables allocated first ... usually only a few large arrays need
  more expensive addressing modes".
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: K. Thompson, "Plan 9 C Compilers", Proc. Summer 1990 UKUUG
%  Conference, London (written for the MIPS, whose loads reach only
%  +-32K from a register: R30 points into the data segment and "the
%  smallest variables [are] allocated first". 5l does the same with R12
%  and the ARM's 12-bit offsets, hence MINSIZ).

### Linker.nw : <<function [[nuxiinit]](arm)>>
(line ~2668, the byte-order part of \subsection{Data section}; see the
author's comment about "nuxi")
Algorithm/idea: byte order (endianness): the linker finds out its host's
  byte order at run time so that it can write target words portably
Verified: find1(0x04030201, i+1) finds where byte i+1 of a long sits in
  the host's memory, which fills the inuxi1/2/4 (and fnuxi) permutation
  tables. datblk() then copies bytes through them. The trick lets 5l run
  on any host and still write little-endian ARM data.
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: D. Cohen, "On Holy Wars and a Plea for Peace", IEEE Computer
%  14(10):48-54, 1981 (the paper that named "big-endian" and
%  "little-endian", after the egg-breaking war in Gulliver's Travels.
%  Unix folklore calls the same thing the "NUXI" problem: "UNIX" stored
%  in 16-bit words on one machine reads back as "NUXI" on another.
%  inuxi4[] records the host's byte permutation).
%ref: R. Pike, "The byte order fallacy", blog post, 2012 (the other
%  approach, which lput()/lputl() already use: build each byte with
%  shifts and never ask what the host's byte order is. The inuxi tables
%  are then unnecessary).

### Linker_x86.nw : <<function [[span]](x86)>>
(line ~2623, \section{x86})
Algorithm/idea: span-dependent instruction sizing by iterating to a
  fixpoint (short 2-byte vs long 6-byte x86 Jcc/JMP)
Verified: doasm() case Zbr emits the 2-byte form when -128 <= v <= 127
  and the 6-byte 0x0f form otherwise. span() assembles everything once,
  then loops over the branches, re-measuring (m != p->mark sets again)
  until no size changes. It stops with "span must be looping" after 50
  iterations, and restarts if the rounding of INITDAT changes. At the
  first pass, forward targets still carry their small virtual pcs, so
  branches start short and grow, as in Szymanski. Unlike his algorithm,
  a size can also shrink, which is why the 50-iteration cap exists.
  Thompson's paper: "Initially, all branches are assumed minimal length.
  On each subsequent pass, the branches are reassessed and expanded if
  necessary [Szy78]".
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: T. G. Szymanski, "Assembling code for machines with span-dependent
%  instructions", CACM 21(4):300-308, 1978 (on machines like the
%  PDP-11, where a short branch reaches only about +-256 bytes: start
%  every branch short, lengthen those out of range, and repeat.
%  Lengthening only pushes code apart, so the loop converges on the
%  smallest program. Szymanski also shows that the problem becomes
%  NP-complete once operands can be expressions such as label
%  differences. The Plan 9 compiler paper cites it as [Szy78] for this
%  very loop. 8l also lets a branch shrink, which is why it caps the
%  loop at 50 iterations).

### Linker_x86.nw : <<function [[dostkoff]](x86)>>
(line ~399, commented-out section "[[dostkoff()]]")

Verified: dostkoff() records, for each TEXT, the argument space it uses

CONFIDENCE: high (Steele); medium (the Alef manual's exact title and

%  or, procedure call implementations considered harmful, or, LAMBDA:
%  the ultimate GOTO", Proc. ACM Annual Conference, 1977, pp. 153-162
%  (a call in tail position can reuse its caller's frame and become a
%  jump. That is Alef's "become", and it is why dostkoff() must make the

### Linker_x86.nw : <<function [[elf32]]>>
(line ~5904, \subsection*{[[linkers/8l/elf.c]]})
Algorithm/idea: the ELF executable format (ELF header, program headers
  for the loader, section headers for tools)
Verified: elf32() writes the ELF identification (elfident), one program
  header per segment for exec, and a minimal section table (elf32sectab)
  with only .text, .data and .strtab. There are no dynamic sections and
  no relocations: a static ELF for booting or running on Linux. The
  section table comes after text+data+syms. On the ARM side, 5l reaches
  this code through the H_ELF case of asmb().
CONFIDENCE: high
Proposed lines:
%ref: TIS Committee, "Tool Interface Standard (TIS) Executable and
%  Linking Format (ELF) Specification", Version 1.2, 1995 (ELF, from
%  System V Release 4, keeps two views of a file: program headers say
%  what exec must map, which is all a static executable needs, and
%  section headers are for linkers and debuggers. elf32() writes the
%  first view in full and only a token .text/.data/.strtab of the second).


##########################################################################
Considered but rejected
##########################################################################
- Ziv-Lempel 1978 (LZ78) for 5a/5l's circular 50-entry object symbol
  table h[] (zname/ANAME): only an analogy. The dictionary updates are
  explicit ANAME records, not implicit, so it would mislead.
- Literal pools (addpool/flushpool): IBM 360 LTORG-style literal tables
  have no founding paper. Salomon's book probably covers literals, but I
  could not give a section, so I left it out.
- immrot() (ARM rotated 8-bit immediates): only the ARM ARM (key
  arm-refman, already cited in Intro.nw). No academic source.
- ARM conditional execution / if-conversion (Allen, Kennedy, Porterfield,
  Warren, POPL 1983): 5a only parses the suffix, and the conversion
  happens in 5c, not here.
- Granlund-Montgomery 1994 (division by invariant integers) for the ADIV
  rewriting: 5l just calls _div. The algorithm belongs in the Compiler or
  Libcore book.
- Clinger 1990 "How to read floating point numbers accurately" for 5a's
  float lexing: 5a calls atof(), so it belongs in Libcore.
- Strachey 1965 GPM (general purpose macrogenerator): a good classic, but
  McIlroy 1960 comes first and is the one about assembler macros. One ref
  is enough.
- a.out format (Thompson & Ritchie 1974 CACM): the paper barely describes
  a.out. It is better cited in Intro prose, if anywhere.
- Leverett & Szymanski 1980 "Chaining span-dependent jump instructions"
  (TOPLAS): 8l does not chain jumps, and Szymanski 1978 is enough.
- Robertson 1979 TOPLAS on span-dependent code generation: not sure of
  the details, so omitted.
- Levine ch. 7/10 for 5l's dynamic-module relocations (dynreloc/asmdyn):
  that feature is obscure and half-dead in the code. Presser-White and
  Daley-Dennis in Intro.nw cover the concepts.
- Knuth Vol. 3 Section 5.2.1 (straight insertion) for dynreloc()'s sorted
  insertion: too trivial to be worth a ref.
- Crelier / Gutknecht (Oberon/Modula-2 symbol-file fingerprints) as a
  second ref for safe linking: close to 5c -T's type hashes, but I was
  not confident enough about the exact citation.
- Plan 9 assembler manual (key plan9-assemblers) for the 8a scaled-index
  syntax: already \cite'd in Intro.nw.
